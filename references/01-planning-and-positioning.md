## 1. Is this publishable? The seven-question test

Ask these before a word is drafted. A "no" or "unclear" on questions 1 to 3 is serious.

1. **So what?** Who will change what they think, do, build, or study because of this? Name the audience.
2. **What is new?** Relative to the three closest existing papers, what exactly do you add?
3. **Is the evidence strong enough for the claim?** Would a skeptical expert believe it from the design alone?
4. **Is the scope right for the venue?** Check recent issues, not just the aims statement.
5. **Is it complete?** Reviewers punish "preliminary" work at selective venues unless the venue is built for it (letters, short communications, workshop papers).
6. **Can it be told simply?** If it takes three paragraphs to explain, the story is not yet found.
7. **Are the ethics, data rights, and authorship settled?**

The Heilmeier-style check also works well: What are you trying to do (no jargon)? How is it done today and what are the limits? What is new in your approach and why might it succeed? Who cares? What risks exist? How will success be measured?

## 2. Framing the research question

- State the question as a precise, answerable sentence. Vague topics ("the effects of X") are not questions.
- Good questions are **specific, important, feasible, and falsifiable** (or, in qualitative and humanistic work, genuinely open and arguable).
- Frame the **gap** as a consequence, not just an absence. "Nobody has studied X" is weak. "We cannot predict Y without knowing X, which limits Z" is strong.
- A useful structure is **problem, what is known, what is unknown and why it matters, what you did**. In many fields this maps onto the Swales CARS model: establish a territory, establish a niche, occupy the niche.
- Beware three weak framings: the **topic-without-claim** (descriptive with no question), the **gap-for-gap's-sake** (a hole nobody needs filled), and the **method-in-search-of-a-problem** (unless it is a methods paper whose value is the method).

## 3. Establishing and defending novelty

Novelty is relative to a specific literature, so establish it by evidence, not assertion.

- Do a **systematic prior-art check**: search by concept, method, and result in the field's main databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar, arXiv, ACM DL, IEEE Xplore, SSRN, JSTOR, as applicable). Check forward citations of the closest papers.
- Write a **novelty table** for yourself: closest 3 to 5 papers in rows; columns for question, method, data, result, limitation; then your row. The differences are your contribution.
- Do not claim "first" unless you have searched exhaustively. Prefer "to our knowledge" with a defensible search, or describe the specific new element instead.
- Beware **incremental-only** framing ("we extend X to Y"). Make the extension meaningful by showing what breaks, what is surprising, or what it enables.
- Anticipate the "this is obvious" objection: show a result that was not predictable, or a prediction that competing theories disagree on.
- Anticipate the "this is known" objection: engage the closest work directly in the introduction instead of hoping reviewers do not know it.

## 4. Types of contribution (state which one you are making)

- **Empirical finding:** a new, reliable result.
- **Method or tool:** a technique or resource others can use; needs benchmark comparisons and availability.
- **Theory or model:** explains or predicts; needs derivation, assumptions, tests or implications.
- **Data or resource:** a dataset, corpus, or instrument; needs documentation, validation, access, and a clear use case.
- **Synthesis:** review or meta-analysis that organizes a field and exposes unresolved questions.
- **Replication or refutation:** sharpens or overturns existing claims; needs adequate power and fidelity.
- **Negative or null result:** valuable when the question mattered and the study was well powered.
- **Perspective or conceptual argument:** reframes a field; must be argued tightly with evidence.

Each type has its own reviewer expectations. Say which you are making in the introduction and in the cover letter.

## 5. Scoping: one paper or several?

- **Too much in one paper** produces diffuse messages and reviewers who find a weakness somewhere. Signs: more than one central claim, methods that need three pages, results in more than five main figures.
- **Too little** (salami slicing, minimal publishable units from one dataset) damages reputation and is an integrity concern when it hides overlap. Split only when the questions are genuinely distinct and each stands alone, and cite and disclose the related papers.
- A reliable rule: **one central claim per paper**, with all evidence organized to support it.
- Early in a career, aim for fewer, stronger papers. Later, build a coherent **research program** where each paper opens the next.

## 6. Planning the paper around the results

1. List all results. Pick the **three to five** that carry the central claim.
2. Draft the main figures and tables first. If the figures tell the story in order, the paper is nearly outlined.
3. Write a **one-paragraph abstract draft** and a **one-sentence contribution statement**.
4. Write a **skeleton outline**, one bullet per paragraph, each stating its point.
5. Identify the **weakest link** in the argument and decide now whether to strengthen it (extra experiment, robustness check, alternative analysis) or to disclose it.
6. Decide what goes to supplementary material: robustness checks, extended methods, secondary analyses, detailed derivations.

## 7. Authorship, order, and contributions

Settle this at the **start** of the project and revisit at milestones. Late disputes are corrosive.

- **ICMJE criteria** (widely used, especially in biomedicine): authors must (1) make substantial contributions to conception, design, or data acquisition, analysis, or interpretation; (2) draft or critically revise the work; (3) give final approval; and (4) agree to be accountable for the work. All four are required. People who do not meet them are acknowledged, not listed.
- **CRediT taxonomy** has 14 contributor roles (Conceptualization, Data curation, Formal analysis, Funding acquisition, Investigation, Methodology, Project administration, Resources, Software, Supervision, Validation, Visualization, Writing original draft, Writing review and editing). Many journals ask for it. Using it makes contributions transparent.
- **Order conventions differ by field:** in many life sciences the first author did most of the work and the last is the senior or supervising author; in mathematics, theoretical computer science, and some economics fields, authors are often alphabetical; in physics and large collaborations, authorship lists can be long and alphabetical or by contribution. Know your field's norm and the target journal's.
- **Corresponding author** handles submission and post-publication queries; it is an administrative role, not a prestige ranking, though it is often treated as such.
- Get an **ORCID** iD for every author. Ensure affiliations, funding, and conflicts of interest are accurate and agreed.
- Never add honorary or "gift" authors, and never omit someone who qualifies (ghost authorship).

## 8. Time and project management

- Write **throughout** the project, not after. Keep a living methods log and results log.
- Protect regular writing time (many senior authors write short sessions daily). Draft fast, revise slowly.
- Use **version control** (Git, or at minimum dated filenames and shared tracked-changes documents) and a **reference manager** (Zotero, Mendeley, EndNote). For math-heavy or CS work, LaTeX with Overleaf or a local setup is standard.
- Set **internal deadlines** for each draft stage and for sharing with co-authors. Give co-authors realistic time and a specific request ("check the Methods for accuracy", not "any thoughts?").
- Plan **three to five readers** before submission: one in your subfield, one outside it, one methodologist or statistician, and one who is a harsh critic.

## 9. Negative, null, and replication results

- Frame them around the **importance of the question**, not the disappointment of the result.
- Show the study was **adequately powered**; report confidence intervals and equivalence bounds so "no effect" is quantified.
- Consider **registered reports** (in-principle acceptance before data collection) at journals that offer them, and venues dedicated to null or replication work.
- A preprint, a data paper, or a thorough OSF repository can still make the work citable and useful.

## 10. When to kill or pivot a paper

- If after honest effort the central claim cannot be supported, **downgrade the claim** to what the data show, or **pivot** to the question the data actually answer.
- If a better paper exists inside the project, extract it.
- If a flaw is fatal and fixing it costs more than starting over, stop. Sunk cost is the enemy of good science.
- Do not stretch weak evidence to reach a publishable-looking result. That path leads to retractions.
