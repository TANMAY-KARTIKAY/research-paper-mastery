## 1. Goals: clarity, concision, precision

Scientific prose succeeds when readers understand the first time. Aim for the **simplest words and structure that carry the exact meaning**. Complexity of thought is not complexity of sentence. Reviewers reward clarity because it makes the work easier to evaluate and signals command of the subject.

## 2. Sentence architecture (Gopen and Swan principles)

Readers expect information in predictable places. Respect those expectations.

1. **Keep subject and verb close.** Long gaps between them force the reader to hold information in memory.
   - Weak: "The enzyme, which was purified from tissue samples collected over three years from patients who had undergone surgery, inhibited growth."
   - Better: "The enzyme inhibited growth. We purified it from tissue samples collected from surgical patients over three years."
2. **Put old (familiar) information at the start of a sentence and new information at the end** (the stress position). The end of a sentence is where the reader expects the emphasis.
3. **Put the main point in the stress position.** Rewrite so the most important new information lands last.
4. **Put the actor in the subject position and the action in the verb.** Avoid hiding verbs in nouns (nominalizations).
5. **Place context before new content** to prepare the reader.
6. **One unit of discourse, one point.** Each sentence and paragraph makes one point.

## 3. Paragraph flow and cohesion

- **Topic sentence first.** The reader should know the paragraph's point from sentence one.
- **Chain sentences** so the end of one connects to the start of the next ("...increased heterogeneity. This heterogeneity complicates...").
- Use **logical connectors** that express real relationships: because, however, therefore, in contrast, for example. Do not sprinkle "moreover" and "furthermore" mechanically.
- Maintain **consistent terms**: do not alternate synonyms for a technical concept ("participants", "subjects", "respondents"), because readers may think they are different things.
- Vary sentence length. Short sentences emphasize; longer sentences develop relationships. A long run of long sentences or of short ones fatigues the reader.
- Make the **structure visible**: signposts ("First", "Second") and parallel structure for parallel ideas.

## 4. Word-level choices

- Prefer **concrete, specific verbs** over "be" plus abstract noun ("The model predicts" instead of "The model is a predictor").
- Cut **filler**: "it is worth noting that", "it should be mentioned that", "in order to" (use "to"), "due to the fact that" (use "because"), "a number of" (use "several" or the number), "has the ability to" (use "can").
- Prefer **plain words**: "use" not "utilize", "show" not "demonstrate" (unless it is a demonstration), "help" not "facilitate".
- **Quantify** vague terms: "large", "many", "significantly" (reserve for statistical significance), "recently", "substantial" should give numbers or ranges.
- Avoid **intensifiers and empty adjectives**: "very", "extremely", "highly", "novel", "robust", "interesting", "important" (show, don't tell).
- Avoid **stacked nouns** ("patient treatment outcome assessment framework"). Use prepositions to untangle.
- Avoid **mixed metaphors** and cliché. Avoid unexplained **jargon** and in-group slang.
- Use "**which**" for nonrestrictive and "**that**" for restrictive clauses (in American usage); check ambiguous pronouns ("this", "it", "they") and give "this" a noun ("this effect", "this approach").

## 5. Voice, tense, and person

- **Active voice** is clearer and shorter ("We measured X", not "X was measured"), and many journals now prefer it, including first person ("we"). **Passive voice** is acceptable when the actor is irrelevant or the object is the focus ("Samples were centrifuged at 4 C").
- **Tense conventions:**
  - **Past** for what you did and found (Methods and Results) and for specific past studies ("Smith et al. reported").
  - **Present** for established facts, general truths, and statements about the paper itself ("Figure 2 shows...", "The data suggest...").
  - **Present perfect** for the evolving state of knowledge ("Several studies have examined...").
  - Keep tense consistent within a section unless meaning requires change.
- **Person:** "we" for multiple authors, "I" for a single author where the field permits. Avoid "the authors" for yourself. Follow journal and field norms (some humanities and social sciences prefer first person strongly; some engineering venues still lean passive).

## 6. Hedging and calibrated claims

Calibrate certainty to evidence, using **graded verbs and modal language**, and avoid both overclaiming and mush.

| Strength of evidence | Typical phrasing |
|---|---|
| Strong, replicated, causal design | "demonstrates", "shows", "establishes" |
| Solid but single study or observational | "indicates", "supports", "is consistent with", "is associated with" |
| Suggestive or exploratory | "suggests", "may", "might", "raises the possibility" |
| Speculation | "we speculate", "one possibility is" (label it as speculation) |

- Hedge **once, in the right place**, not with a pile ("might possibly suggest that it may be").
- Avoid overclaims: "proves" (almost never in empirical work), "always", "never", "clearly shows" (if clearly, it needs no adverb), "novel" (let the reader judge), "first" (unless verified), "causes" (without causal design).
- Avoid underclaiming that hides real findings.
- Be direct about uncertainty: report intervals and say what the data cannot rule out.

## 7. Common wordiness patterns and fixes

| Pattern | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Nominalization | "We performed an analysis of the data" | "We analyzed the data" |
| Weak opener | "There are several factors that affect X" | "Several factors affect X" |
| Redundant pair | "each and every", "basic fundamentals", "completely eliminate" | "each", "fundamentals", "eliminate" |
| Throat-clearing | "It is interesting to note that" | Delete; state the point |
| Doubled prepositions | "in the case of", "with respect to" | "in", "for", "about" |
| Passive hiding actor | "It was decided that..." | "We decided..." |
| Starting with "The fact that" | "The fact that X occurs suggests..." | "That X occurs suggests..." or "X occurs, suggesting..." |
| Over-long lists | Five clauses joined by commas | Split or tabulate |

Aim to cut **10 to 20 percent** from a first draft without losing content.

## 8. Terminology, abbreviations, and numbers

- **Define abbreviations on first use** in the abstract and again in the main text; use them only if they appear **three or more times** and add value. Avoid inventing acronyms for one-off concepts. Do not start a sentence with an abbreviation if avoidable.
- **Keep terms consistent** across sections; define key constructs precisely, once.
- **Numbers:** spell out numbers below ten in running text when the style requires (check the journal), use numerals with units, and keep significant figures consistent and meaningful. Use SI units, a space between number and unit (follow the style guide), and consistent decimal separators. Do not start a sentence with a numeral.
- Use **italics for statistical symbols** (p, t, F, n) where the style requires, and consistent formatting for equations. Number and refer to equations that are referenced.
- **Capitalization and spelling:** choose American or British spelling and stay consistent.

## 9. Tone and persuasion

- Persuade through **evidence, logic, and clarity**, not adjectives. Let the findings speak.
- Be **fair to competing views**; describe them accurately before critiquing.
- Be **modest but not apologetic**. State what you found and why it matters; state limits plainly.
- Avoid **hype** ("groundbreaking", "revolutionary", "unprecedented") and **defensiveness**. Do not disparage prior work.
- Tell a **coherent narrative** (a frame such as And-But-Therefore: here is what is known; but here is the problem; therefore we did this). Tension and resolution keep readers engaged even in technical writing.

## 10. Writing in English as an additional language

- Journals judge **science**, but unclear language hinders review and can lead to rejection. Invest in clarity.
- Write in **short, direct sentences** with simple structure; avoid idioms and complicated constructions you are unsure of.
- Read **good papers in your journal** and borrow structures and phrases (academic phrasebanks), not content.
- Use **language-editing services** or fluent colleagues, and AI tools carefully within policy, then check that meaning has not changed.
- Learn the **common trouble spots**: articles (a, the), prepositions, countable and uncountable nouns, subject-verb agreement, tense, and pronoun reference.
- **Read aloud** and use text-to-speech to catch awkwardness.
- Do not be embarrassed. Clear, plain English is the target, not literary English.

## 11. Revision process: passes

Revise in layers rather than line by line from page one.

1. **Structure pass:** reverse outline; is each section doing its job; is the argument in order; what is missing or redundant?
2. **Argument and evidence pass:** does each claim have evidence; are alternatives addressed; are claims proportionate?
3. **Paragraph pass:** topic sentences, one idea each, transitions.
4. **Sentence pass:** apply the Gopen and Swan principles; kill filler and nominalizations; vary rhythm.
5. **Consistency pass:** numbers, terms, abbreviations, tense, and formatting across text, tables, and figures.
6. **Cut pass:** remove 10 to 20 percent.
7. **Polish pass:** spelling, punctuation, references, formatting to the journal.
8. **Read aloud and read backward** (last paragraph first) to catch errors.
9. **Cold read:** rest the draft for a few days, then read as a reviewer. Ask a colleague outside your subfield to summarize your contribution in their own words. If their summary is wrong, the paper is unclear.
