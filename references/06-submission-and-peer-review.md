## 1. Choosing the venue

Choose **before** writing; rewriting for a new venue costs weeks.

**Fit criteria (rank them):**
1. **Scope and audience:** read the aims and scope, and **the last 12 months of titles and abstracts**. Do papers like yours appear? Is your audience reading it?
2. **Ambition match:** be realistic about the contribution's significance. Aim one tier above comfortable, not three. Overreaching wastes months; undershooting wastes impact.
3. **Article type and constraints:** word and figure limits, structure, reporting requirements, data policy, and whether it takes your type (replication, null result, methods, data paper).
4. **Speed and process:** median time to first decision and to publication (published on many journals' pages), whether peer review is single- or double-anonymous, and whether the journal transfers papers to sister journals with reviews.
5. **Cost and access:** subscription versus open access, article processing charges (APCs), waivers, and funder requirements.
6. **Reputation and field norms:** what hiring, tenure, and funding bodies in your field value (e.g., conferences are primary in computer science; books in some humanities fields; certain journals in medicine).
7. **Editor and board:** is there an editor with relevant expertise? Past experience with that editor or journal?

**Tools and signals:** your reference list (where do your key citations publish?), publisher journal finders (Elsevier, Springer Nature, Wiley, Taylor and Francis), Jane (biomedical), Scopus Sources, and colleagues' experience.

**Plan A, B, C in advance**, ordered by prestige and fit, with notes on reformatting needs. Move down the list promptly after a rejection.

**Conferences vs journals:** conferences have fixed deadlines and short windows; choose the target 6 to 9 months ahead, track deadlines, and plan the rebuttal period. See `09` for CS/ML.

## 2. Evaluating journal quality and avoiding predators

Metrics are crude. Use them with caution and favor **qualitative judgment** (DORA and the Leiden Manifesto warn against using journal impact factor to judge individual papers).

- **Metrics:** Journal Impact Factor (Clarivate JCR), CiteScore and SJR (Scopus), h5-index (Google Scholar). Compare within field, not across fields.
- **Indexing:** check for Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed/MEDLINE, DOAJ (for open access), and field-specific indexes.
- **Predatory or deceptive journal warning signs:** unsolicited spam invitations with flattering language, promise of very fast review, unclear or hidden fees, fake or misleading metrics, no clear editorial board or fake editors, vague scope, sloppy website, publisher not a COPE or OASPA member, journal name that mimics a respected title, and publisher address or contact inconsistencies.
- Use **Think. Check. Submit.**, DOAJ's list, COPE membership, and ask experienced colleagues. If it is not on trusted indexes and you cannot verify editors, do not submit.
- Beware **paper mills and special issue abuse** that inflate output at the expense of credibility.

## 3. Open access, preprints, and funder mandates

- **Gold OA:** published openly, often with an APC. **Green OA:** deposit an accepted or pre-print version in a repository (check embargo and version policies via Sherpa Romeo). **Hybrid:** subscription journal with optional OA. **Diamond:** free to authors and readers.
- **Funder mandates** (e.g., NIH, Wellcome, Horizon Europe and cOAlition S's Plan S, national agencies) may require immediate open access, specific licenses, or data sharing. Check **before** choosing a journal; transformative agreements at your institution may cover APCs.
- **Preprints** (arXiv, bioRxiv, medRxiv, SSRN, OSF Preprints, ChemRxiv, field-specific servers) establish priority, receive early feedback, and are accepted by most journals. Check the target journal's preprint policy first, and note exceptions (some journals or double-anonymous venues, and in some medical contexts, have constraints). Update the preprint when the paper is published and link to the final version.
- Choose a **license** deliberately (CC BY is common for OA); keep copyright rules in mind.

## 4. Preparing the submission package

Obey the **author guidelines to the letter**: formatting, reference style, word counts, structure, figure formats, line numbers, anonymization, and file naming. Technical rejection for noncompliance wastes weeks.

Typical package:
- Cover letter
- Manuscript (main text, tables, figures; anonymized version if double-blind)
- Title page (authors, affiliations, ORCIDs, corresponding author, word counts, keywords, funding)
- Abstract and keywords, highlights or plain-language summary if requested
- Supplementary materials
- Reporting checklist (CONSORT, PRISMA, etc.) and any required forms (author contributions, conflict-of-interest disclosures, copyright or license agreements)
- Ethics approval and trial or review registration numbers
- Data and code availability statement and links
- Suggested reviewers (with institutional emails and a brief reason) and **excluded reviewers** (with legitimate reason), if allowed
- Statements on AI use, preprint, prior presentation, and related manuscripts

Proofread everything, including metadata typed into the submission portal. Submit only when **all authors have approved** the final version.

## 5. The cover letter

Purpose: help the editor decide quickly that the paper fits and deserves review. One page. It is read by an editor making a triage decision, so it must be informative, specific, and honest, not a duplicate of the abstract or empty hype. See `11-templates.md` for a template.

Include:
1. **Addressee:** the handling editor by name when known; otherwise "Dear Editor".
2. **Title and article type.**
3. **The question and gap** (two sentences), **the main finding or contribution** (two sentences), and **why it matters to this journal's readers**, referencing the journal's scope or recent related papers.
4. **Declarations:** original, not under consideration elsewhere, all authors approved, conflicts of interest and funding stated, ethical approvals, related submissions or preprints disclosed.
5. **Suggested and excluded reviewers**, if asked.
6. Any special considerations: data availability, prior communication with the editor, a pre-submission enquiry.

Avoid: exaggerated language ("groundbreaking", "paradigm shift"), claims of urgency, restating the abstract word for word, and critiques of other labs.

Consider a **pre-submission enquiry** (an abstract and a short note) for selective journals to gauge interest before investing in formatting.

## 6. What happens after submission

1. **Administrative check:** formatting, completeness, ethics, plagiarism screening (similarity software), image integrity checks.
2. **Editor triage:** the editor (or editorial board) decides if it fits scope, is novel and significant enough, and is technically sound enough to merit review. At selective journals a large share of manuscripts are **desk rejected** at this stage, often within days. Clear significance in the title, abstract, cover letter, and first page of the introduction matters most.
3. **Reviewer selection and invitation:** typically two to three reviewers, sometimes more; it can take weeks to find them.
4. **Review:** reviewers assess significance, novelty, validity, clarity, and ethics; they write reports and recommendations to the editor.
5. **Decision:** accept, minor revision, major revision, reject and resubmit, or reject. Many accepted papers pass through at least one revision round.
6. **Production:** copyediting, proofs (check carefully; errors introduced here are on you), publication, and promotion.

## 7. Reading a decision letter

- Read it, then **put it away for a day or two** before reacting. Emotional responses are normal; rebuttals written in anger do harm.
- Identify the **editor's synthesis**: the editor often tells you which concerns are essential. Decisions are made by the editor, not by a vote of reviewers.
- Classify each comment: **essential** (must address, fatal if not), **valid improvement**, **misunderstanding** (your paper was unclear), **out of scope or impractical**, **disagreement**.
- "**Major revision**" is encouraging: the editor sees potential. "**Reject and resubmit**" means substantial rework and a new review. "**Minor revision**" is near acceptance, but still address everything carefully.

## 8. Responding to reviewers (revision strategy)

**Mindset:** reviewers are volunteering their time, and every reviewer comment is a signal about what a reader might think. Your goal is a better paper and a **convinced editor**. Be gracious, rigorous, and evidence-driven.

**Process:**
1. **Triage and plan:** list every comment, group duplicates across reviewers, and decide the response type for each (revise, add analysis, clarify, rebut with evidence, explain why not).
2. **Do the work.** New analyses, experiments, or literature checks beat arguments. If you cannot do an experiment, explain why and add the limitation to the paper.
3. **Revise the manuscript** so the paper itself answers the concern. A comment about clarity means clarify the text, not just reply in the letter.
4. **Write the response letter** (see template in `11`):
   - Start with a **brief thank-you and an overview** of the major changes.
   - **Quote each reviewer comment verbatim** (in a distinct style), then give a **point-by-point response** stating what you did and **where** (page and line numbers, section) the change appears. Show the revised text excerpt where useful.
   - Be **polite, specific, and concise**. Never sarcastic. Acknowledge valid criticism. Do not say "the reviewer misunderstood"; say "we have clarified this point because it was not clear."
   - **Disagree with evidence**, not emotion: cite data, literature, or logic; offer a partial concession or an alternative analysis if you can; explain respectfully.
   - Do not ignore any comment, including minor ones and typos.
   - Mention **new analyses** and results, additional data, or changed conclusions explicitly.
5. **Submit a tracked-changes version and a clean version** if requested, plus the response letter, updated figures, and checklists. Match the editor's deadline or ask for an extension early.
6. **Check for consistency:** if you changed a number or an analysis, update the abstract, tables, figures, and conclusions everywhere.

**Tone examples:**
- Instead of: "The reviewer is wrong." Use: "We thank the reviewer for raising this. We agree that X is important and have added Y. However, our data suggest Z for the following reasons (Fig. S3), and we now state this explicitly on page 8, lines 210 to 218."
- Instead of: "This was already explained." Use: "We have made this point more prominent in the Methods (page 5)."

## 9. Handling difficult reviewer situations

- **Contradictory reviewers:** tell the editor which advice you followed and why, usually in a short note in the letter; choose what best improves the paper and is supported by evidence.
- **Unreasonable requests** (a whole new study, a different research question): explain politely why it is beyond scope, acknowledge the limitation in the paper, and offer what is feasible. Ask the editor if the request is essential.
- **Request to cite the reviewer's work:** cite only if relevant. Otherwise explain politely. Raise coercive citation requests with the editor.
- **Reviewer seems unqualified or biased:** document concrete factual or methodological errors and respond to the substance; politely flag concerns to the editor and request an additional reviewer if justified.
- **Possible misconduct** by a reviewer (confidentiality breach, conflict): inform the editor and consult COPE guidance.
- **Reviewer demands a changed conclusion not supported by data:** hold your ground with evidence, soften the language if the data allow only that.

## 10. Rejection: what to do

1. **Wait 24 to 48 hours.** Everyone gets rejected, including senior researchers many times.
2. **Extract the information:** which critiques are valid and fixable? Which are about fit or ambition (so a different venue could suit)?
3. **Fix real issues** before resubmitting elsewhere; the same flaws will be found by the next reviewers, and reviewers sometimes overlap between journals.
4. **Move quickly**: resubmit within days to a few weeks. Use your Plan B.
5. **Use transfer services** (many publishers offer manuscript transfer with reviews to sister journals) to save time.
6. **Calibrate the tier:** after repeated desk rejections, the issue is usually significance or fit; after review-based rejections, it is usually rigor or clarity.
7. Do not submit the same paper simultaneously to multiple journals (it violates ethics rules).

## 11. Appeals

Appeals rarely succeed and are warranted only for **clear factual or procedural errors** (reviewer misread a central analysis, evident conflict of interest, a review that did not engage the paper).
- Write a short, respectful letter to the editor with specific evidence and a clear request (reconsideration, new reviewer).
- Do not appeal merely because you disagree with the assessment or because the decision stings.
- If the appeal fails, move on to another venue with improvements.

## 12. Serving as a reviewer

Reviewing teaches more about writing than anything else. Be the reviewer you wish you had.

- **Accept only if you have the expertise, time, and no conflict.** Declare conflicts. Treat the manuscript as **confidential**: do not share it, and do not upload it to AI tools or public platforms unless the journal explicitly allows. Do not use ideas from it.
- **Read the whole paper and supplements**, then write:
  1. **Summary** of the paper's aims, methods, and claims (shows you understood it).
  2. **Overall assessment** of significance, novelty, and soundness.
  3. **Major concerns:** issues that affect the validity or interpretation, with specific suggestions or analyses that would resolve them.
  4. **Minor concerns:** clarity, presentation, missing references, typos.
  5. **Confidential comments to the editor** (recommendation, concerns about ethics or integrity).
- Be **specific, constructive, and civil**. Critique the work, not the authors. Number the points so authors can respond.
- Separate **what is wrong** from **what you would have done differently**.
- Ask for **evidence**, not for a different paper. Distinguish essential from optional requests.
- Check **ethics, data availability, statistics, and reporting guideline compliance**.
- Meet the deadline or tell the editor early.

## 13. Timeline expectations and follow-up

- Timelines vary widely: days for desk decisions; commonly several weeks to a few months for review; faster in some fields and slower in others.
- If there is no decision well beyond the journal's stated median (often after about three months, but check the journal), send a **brief, polite status enquiry** to the editorial office.
- Keep a **submission tracker** (journal, date, status, decision date, reviewer comments, next steps) and a pipeline of papers in different stages.
