## 1. Searching the literature systematically

- Combine **keyword searches** (with synonyms, spelling variants, and controlled vocabulary such as MeSH) with **citation chaining**: look at the references of key papers (backward) and at who cites them (forward, via Google Scholar, Scopus, Web of Science, or Semantic Scholar).
- Use the field's core databases: PubMed or Embase (biomedicine), PsycINFO (psychology), ERIC (education), EconLit and RePEc (economics), IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, DBLP and arXiv (computing), MathSciNet and zbMATH (mathematics), JSTOR and Project MUSE (humanities), SSRN and OSF Preprints, plus bioRxiv and medRxiv for preprints.
- Set **alerts** for authors, keywords, and key papers so the search stays current through the long life of a paper.
- **Search again just before submission.** Papers that ignore a very recent, closely related study look careless, and can be scooped.
- Search in **adjacent fields** and under different terminology; the same idea often appears under another name.
- For a formal review, document the search (databases, strings, dates, filters) so it can be reproduced and reported (PRISMA).

## 2. Reading efficiently and keeping notes

- Use a **multi-pass approach**: first pass (title, abstract, figures, conclusion) to decide relevance; second pass (introduction, key results, limitations) to understand; third pass (methods and details) only for papers central to your work.
- Record for each key paper: question, method, data, main result, limitation, and **how it relates to your work**. A synthesis table (papers in rows; themes in columns) shows consensus, conflict, and gaps at a glance.
- Read critically: what would change my mind about this paper's claim? What did they not test?
- Distinguish **primary sources** from reviews and secondary citations. Cite the original when you make a claim about its findings, and read what you cite.

## 3. Synthesizing instead of listing

Weak: "Smith (2019) found X. Jones (2020) found Y. Lee (2021) found Z." (a list)
Strong: "Evidence on X is mixed: studies using method A report positive effects (Smith 2019; Lee 2021), whereas those using method B find none (Jones 2020), possibly because of differences in Q."

Principles:
- Organize **by idea, tension, or question**, not by author or chronology (unless history is the point).
- Compare and contrast, explain **why** findings differ (population, method, power, definitions), and identify what remains unresolved.
- Make the **gap emerge** from the synthesis.
- Use citations to **support claims**, not to display reading.

## 4. Positioning your work

- Place your contribution **against the closest prior work**, not the distant field. Name it and say how you differ and why it matters.
- Acknowledge work that **overlaps or competes**. Silence invites reviewers to supply it, and not in your favor.
- Use verbs that fairly characterize prior work ("reported", "argued", "estimated") and avoid sneering ("failed to", "ignored") unless warranted by evidence.
- Align your terminology with the field's accepted terms, or justify new ones. Coined jargon without need irritates reviewers.
- Show awareness of **both sides** of a debate.

## 5. Citation practice: what, how much, and how

- **Cite for:** claims of fact not common knowledge, methods and tools you used, data sources, prior findings you build on or contest, theories and definitions, and statistics from other sources.
- **Do not cite for:** common knowledge, your own claims supported by your data, or as decoration.
- **How many:** follow field norms and venue limits. Original articles often have 30 to 60 references, reviews many more, letters fewer. Quality and relevance beat count.
- **Recency and balance:** include **seminal** works and **recent** (last few years) work. A reference list with nothing from the last three years suggests you are not current.
- **Primary over secondary:** cite the original study; use reviews for broad background.
- **Specific citation:** attach each citation to the exact claim it supports. Page numbers for direct quotes.
- Paraphrase in your own words; quote sparingly, with quotation marks and a page number, when exact wording matters.
- Follow the journal's **citation style** exactly (numbered Vancouver or IEEE; author-date APA, Harvard, Chicago, MLA, or others) and its reference list format, including DOIs.

## 6. Citing fairly: reviewers, rivals, and non-English work

- Cite **leaders in the specific subfield**, including those likely to review you, because honest recognition of relevant work is both ethical and prudent. Do not cite someone merely to flatter a reviewer, and never at the request of an editor or reviewer if the work is not relevant (see 8).
- Cite **findings that contradict yours** and explain the discrepancy.
- Cite important work from **under-represented regions and non-English literature** when relevant, and diversify beyond the same few groups and journals.
- Give credit to **data and software** creators; cite datasets, code, and packages (many have preferred citations).

## 7. Reference management and accuracy

- Use **Zotero, Mendeley, EndNote, or BibTeX** from the first day; store DOIs; keep a clean library and avoid manual formatting.
- **Verify every reference** against the source: authors, year, title, journal, volume, pages, DOI. Reference errors are common and avoidable. Check that retractions have not occurred (look up in Retraction Watch or the publisher's page).
- Ensure **every in-text citation appears in the list and vice versa**.
- Keep one **master library** and per-project collections. Back up regularly.
- For preprints, cite the **published version** when it exists, and label preprints clearly otherwise.

## 8. Citation ethics and red flags

- **Never fabricate or guess references**, including when using AI tools, which can generate plausible but nonexistent citations. Check every reference exists and says what you claim.
- Avoid **excessive self-citation**. Cite your own work only where relevant, and be ready to justify it.
- Resist **coercive citation**: if an editor or reviewer demands citations to their own or their journal's work without scholarly reason, politely decline and, if it persists, raise it with the editor or the publisher.
- Do not participate in **citation cartels** or reciprocal citation schemes.
- Do not cite retracted papers as valid evidence (cite them only to note the retraction if necessary).
- Avoid **citation laundering** (citing a review for a claim whose original source says otherwise) and **citing papers you have not read**.
- Avoid text recycling and close paraphrase without attribution (see `07`).

## 9. Writing a literature review section or article

**Within an original paper:** brief, argumentative, and aimed at the gap. Do not write a mini textbook.

**Stand-alone narrative review:**
1. Define the scope and why a review is needed now.
2. Describe the search approach (even narrative reviews should say how sources were found).
3. Organize by themes, controversies, or conceptual frameworks, with a synthesis table or conceptual figure.
4. Evaluate quality and bias of evidence, not only summarize.
5. Identify gaps and propose an agenda or framework (this is what makes reviews cited).

**Systematic review or meta-analysis:** register a protocol (PROSPERO or OSF), follow PRISMA 2020, document search strings and screening (flow diagram), use predefined inclusion criteria, assess risk of bias (RoB 2, ROBINS-I, Newcastle-Ottawa, or field-appropriate tools), quantify heterogeneity (I-squared, tau-squared, prediction intervals), examine publication bias (funnel plots, tests), assess certainty (GRADE), and report sensitivity analyses.

**Scoping review:** map evidence breadth with PRISMA-ScR; no quality synthesis required but a clear question and charting method are.

## 10. Citation checklist

- [ ] Every non-obvious claim has a source, and the source says what I claim.
- [ ] The closest competing and complementary work is cited and discussed.
- [ ] Recent literature (last 2 to 3 years) is included, and I searched again recently.
- [ ] Primary sources are cited rather than only reviews or secondary reports.
- [ ] References are formatted to the target journal style with DOIs.
- [ ] In-text citations and the reference list match exactly.
- [ ] No retracted or unverifiable sources.
- [ ] Self-citations are justified and proportionate.
- [ ] Data, code, and materials are cited.
- [ ] Quotes are minimal, quoted, and paged.
